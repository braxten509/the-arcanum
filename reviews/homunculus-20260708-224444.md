# Homunculus — Course Review (2026-07-08)

Reviewed against `course-configuration-guide.md` and `course-improvement-guide.md`. Scope: all 12 sections (`s11`, `s12`, `s01`–`s10` — narrative order, per `tome.toml [content] sections`), `tome.toml`, `badges.toml`, `shop.toml`, `themes.toml`, `attacks_src.toml`, `intrusions.toml`. Special focus per the player's request: **can a beginner, or someone rusty on Python, actually finish this?**

## Bottom line up front

**Mostly yes, with one real gap and one structural risk.** The course is unusually well-built for the "no prior Python" promise in `tome.toml`'s `description`: Chapters I–II (`s11`, `s12`) teach real Python from `print()` through comprehensions with zero unexplained jargon, and every later chapter (`s01`–`s10`) deliberately keeps the *graded, typed* Python at that same Chapter I–II level (dicts, loops, f-strings, comprehensions) even while the *reading material* races ahead into ROCm/QLoRA/RAG/quantization territory. That "exercises stay simple, prose carries the domain content" pattern is applied consistently and is the course's biggest strength. It is not, however, applied perfectly — see finding 1.

The bigger risk for a true beginner isn't the Python, it's the domain: this is an AI/ML engineering course wearing a "Python from scratch" cover story. A reader with zero ML/GPU background will be asked to take a lot of transformer-internals math (KV-cache formulas, Adam optimizer memory, LoRA rank/alpha math) largely on faith, chapter after chapter, with shrinking support (see finding 2).

## Findings, most important first

### 1. One genuinely new Python feature is taught ad hoc, mid-exercise, with no lesson of its own
`s04/lessons/l04.toml`, exercise 6, introduces **list slice assignment** (`labels[start:end] = token_ids[start:end]`) — a construct never covered in `s11`/`s12` — inside a single write-exercise's starter comment ("NEW LIST TOOLS (beyond Chapters I-II)"), rather than teaching it in the lesson body first. Every other new-tool introduction in the course (e.g. `sum(d.values())` in s03-l01, `sorted(seq, key=f)` in s01-l03, `min()`/`.index()` in s05-l05) gets an opening-callout explanation before the student is asked to use it in a graded exercise; this one doesn't. For a true beginner this is the one spot where the "stays at Ch I-II level" promise is broken without warning.
**Fix:** add a sentence or two on slice assignment to `s04/lessons/l04.toml`'s lesson body (or its opening callout) before exercise 6 uses it.

### 2. Support scaffolding (readings, `essential` flags) thins out exactly where domain difficulty increases
- `s11`/`s12` (pure Python): nearly every lesson has a `[[lessons.readings]]` entry **and** exactly one marked `essential = true`.
- `s01`–`s09`: readings exist but **no lesson anywhere outside s11/s12 has an `essential = true` flag** — the reader is never told which of two readings is the load-bearing one once the material turns to ML/GPU topics. Several lessons have zero readings at all: `s01-l04`, `s02-l02/l04/l05`, `s03-l01/l03/l04/l06`, `s04-l02/l03/l04/l05`, `s05-l02/l03/l04/l05/l06`.
- `s10` (the capstone "living pipeline" chapter): **no lesson has a `body_words` field or a `readings` section at all** — a structural regression relative to every other chapter (s06–s09 all have both on every lesson).

Per the improvement guide, "the load-bearing [reading] should be flagged `essential = true`" and missing readings should be added — this is exactly the rubric's #3 priority, and it's inverted here: the chapters most in need of an anchor doc (QLoRA math, RAG retrieval, alignment/RLHF) are the ones with the least reading support.
**Fix:** add at least one reading to every lesson in `s10`, and mark one `essential = true` reading per lesson in `s01`–`s09` (the highest-value candidate is usually already listed — e.g. the QLoRA arXiv link in `s05-l01`, the bitsandbytes ROCm doc in `s03-l05`).

### 3. Domain-difficulty ramp is steep and un-signposted for a true ML beginner
The course's own opening (`s11` brief) promises a reader who "has never written a line of Python," and the Python delivery genuinely earns that. But starting at `s01` the *conceptual* content — gfx1201/wavefront GPU internals, KV-cache and Adam-optimizer memory formulas, NF4/QLoRA math, loss masking with `-100` sentinels, Jaccard similarity, embeddings/FAISS, RLHF/DPO/abliteration — is graduate-level ML systems material, delivered in dense multi-hundred-word lesson bodies with worked examples but no prerequisite check. A "rusty on Python" adult will be fine. A true first-time-programmer with no ML/hardware background will be able to *pass* every exercise (because the graded code stays arithmetic/dict/loop-level) while genuinely not understanding *why* the numbers mean what they mean — the course is more honest about being a "Python bridge into ML engineering" than its one-line `description` in `tome.toml` lets on.
**Fix (lower cost than it sounds):** nothing needs re-architecting — just soften the `tome.toml [meta] description`/`[narrative] bootLines` framing so it doesn't oversell "no prior Python" as "no prior anything." A single added clause (e.g., "...no prior Python required — some comfort with the idea of GPUs and machine learning will help") sets expectations correctly without touching any graded content.

### 4. Minor grading-surface risks (spot-checked, not confirmed failures)
- `s02-l05` exercise 2 (`text` type) accepts loose phrasing like "which checks failed" — text/fill exercises elsewhere in the course tend toward tighter accept-lists, so this one is more subjective than its siblings and could be graded inconsistently depending on how lenient the grader's string matching is.
- `s04-l05` exercise 4 (`fill`) expects a verbatim regex string (`r'[A-Za-z0-9+/]{32,}={0,2}'`) as the answer — an unforgiving exact-match target for a fill-in-the-blank; a student who writes a functionally-equivalent-but-differently-spaced regex would likely be marked wrong.
- `s02-l05` exercise 6 asks the student to recall a phrase from the section's own `freestyle.toml` rubric prose rather than a Python or ML skill — an unusual "test the syllabus" question that doesn't fit the exercise-type rubric (mc/write/type/text) described in the configuration guide.

These are all low-severity and don't block completion, but worth a look during the next iteration pass.

### 5. Shop item ordering is cosmetic-inconsistent (not a bug, but worth noting since it was flagged in the survey)
`shop.toml`'s consumables aren't cost-sorted in file order (450, 600, 700, 800, then 500) — `xray` (500) is cheaper than `x2` (600) but listed after `vpn` (800). Purely a readability nit for whoever edits the shop next; doesn't affect players since the UI presumably sorts or the order doesn't matter functionally.

## What's already strong (don't disturb this)

- **s11/s12 are excellent true-beginner material.** Every construct (print, variables, types, strings, arithmetic, if/elif, functions, lists, for/while, dicts, comprehensions) is introduced from zero, with worked examples before every exercise uses it. This is the strongest two chapters in the tome and the right foundation for the "no prior Python" claim.
- **The "exercises stay simple, prose carries the domain" design** is applied consistently from `s01` through `s10` (with the one exception in finding 1) via opening-chapter callouts reassuring the reader the Python won't get harder than Ch I–II. This is a genuinely good pedagogical choice for a mixed-background audience and should be preserved in any future edits.
- **Hint coverage is 100%** — every one of the ~186 exercises across all 33 lesson files has a non-empty `hint`. This is the improvement guide's #2 priority and it's already fully satisfied.
- **`whyWrong`/`explain` correctness on MC exercises is also 100%** — no mismatched answer indices or contradicted distractors were found anywhere.
- **All hand-checkable arithmetic in `write` exercises across the quantitative chapters (s03, s05, s06–s10) was independently recomputed and is correct** — no wrong `expect` strings found.
- **All `[[rubric]]` weights across every `freestyle.toml` (s11, s12, s01–s10) sum to exactly 100.**
- **`themes.toml` fully complies with the validator's hard rules**: all 18 vars present on all 4 palettes, `candle` is a bare RGB triple everywhere (never hex), `bg1` reads as warm paper on every theme regardless of how dark/saturated `bg0` gets.
- **No TODO/FIXME/lorem/placeholder text found anywhere** in any of the 33 lesson files or the 6 top-level config files surveyed.
- **`attacks_src.toml` (18 challenges, 6 tiers) and `intrusions.toml` (15 pool entries, 5 tiers)** are both well-populated with real, arithmetically-verified content — no thin spots.

## Summary

The course delivers on its core promise better than most tomes of this size: `s11`/`s12` teach Python to a genuine zero-experience reader, and every later chapter is careful to keep graded exercises at that same beginner level even as the lesson prose moves into serious ML/GPU territory — hints and MC feedback are complete, and all rubric math checks out. The two things worth fixing are narrower than they sound: one exercise (`s04-l04-e6`) introduces a new Python feature without teaching it first, and reading/`essential`-flag support thins out (and vanishes entirely in `s10`) exactly where the domain content gets hardest — both are small, targeted additions, not a redesign. The real caveat for the player's question is that "beginner" in this course means beginner-to-Python, not beginner-to-ML: someone with zero GPU/machine-learning background will be able to pass every exercise but will be taking a lot of the underlying math on faith, so tempering the "no prior Python required" framing to acknowledge that would set expectations more honestly.
